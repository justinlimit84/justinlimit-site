7.1 Work done by a constant force

In science, work is done on an object when a force acts on it and the object is displaced in the direction of that force. Lifting one bag through 1 m is some work. Lifting three bags, or lifting one bag three times as high, is three times the work. So work grows with the force and with the displacement:

W = F × s work = force × displacement in the direction of the force
  • SI unit: the joule (J). 1 J = 1 N × 1 m = 1 kg m2 s−2. That is the work done when a force of 1 newton moves an object by 1 metre in the direction of the force.
  • Always say which force (who or what is pushing) does work on which object.
  • Work has no direction, but it can be positive or negative.
  • On a force–displacement graph, work = area under the graph. This works even when the force changes along the way.

When is the work zero?

  • No force (F = 0): no work.
  • No displacement (s = 0): no work, however hard you push. Pushing a wall does no work on the wall, even though you get tired. Your muscles use up your body's energy as they tense and relax.
  • Force perpendicular to the displacement: no work by that force. When you carry a bag while walking, your upward force does no work on the bag, because the bag moves sideways.

Positive and negative work

  • Positive work: displacement in the same direction as the force (pushing a wheelchair forward).
  • Negative work: displacement opposite to the force (a goalkeeper's hands stopping a ball). In W = F × s, take s as negative.

7.2–7.3 Energy and the work–energy theorem

A thrown cricket ball can knock the wicket over. A flower pot raised high can break something if it falls. Each has gained the capacity to do work, and anything with the capacity to do work has energy. The ball got its energy from the work the fielder did in throwing it. The pot got its energy from the work done in lifting it.

work done on an object = change in its energy the work–energy theorem (it holds for systems of objects, and for forces that are not constant)

Energy is measured in the same SI unit as work, the joule (J). Positive work done on an object increases its energy, and negative work decreases it. Energy can change from one form to another:

FormWhat it isExample of a change
Mechanicaldue to motion or positiona ringing bell: mechanical → sound
Thermalmakes things warm or hotelectric water heater: electrical → thermal
Lightlets us seebulb: electrical → light (and heat)
Soundvibrations of air or other materialmicrophone: sound → electrical
Electricalrelated to positions or motion of chargesfan: electrical → mechanical
Chemicalstored in the bonds between atoms (food, fuels)muscles: chemical → mechanical
Nuclearstored in the nuclei of atomsthe Sun: nuclear → light and heat
Beyond the book's core

Doing work is only one way to transfer energy. Energy also moves as heat (hot to cold), by radiation (sunlight), in electric circuits and in sound waves.

7.4 Mechanical energy: kinetic and potential

Mechanical energy is energy an object has because of its motion (kinetic energy) or its position or shape (potential energy).

Kinetic energy

Kinetic energy is the energy an object has because it is moving. Here is where the formula comes from, in four steps. Take a constant force F acting on a mass m, which speeds up from u to v over a displacement s:

  1. From the equations of motion: v2 = u2 + 2as, so s = (v2 − u2) / 2a.
  2. Work done by the force: W = F × s, and Newton's second law gives F = ma.
  3. So W = ma × (v2 − u2) / 2a = ½ m(v2 − u2). The a cancels!
  4. Starting from rest (u = 0), all of this work becomes kinetic energy:
K = ½ m v2 kinetic energy, in joules; it has no direction

Positive work speeds an object up, so its kinetic energy grows. Negative work slows it down. No work means the speed and the kinetic energy stay the same. Because v is squared, doubling the speed makes the kinetic energy four times bigger.

Potential energy

Potential energy is energy stored because of an object's shape (a stretched rubber band, a bent bow, a compressed spring) or because of the positions of objects in a system that pull or push on each other (two magnets, two charges, a ball and the Earth). Released, that stored energy can do work.

To lift a mass m slowly to a height h, you apply an upward force equal to its weight mg, so you do work mg × h. We take the potential energy on the ground as zero, so:

U = m g h gravitational potential energy at a height h above the ground (valid near the Earth's surface)

Conservation of mechanical energy

Mechanical energy = kinetic energy + potential energy. Drop a ball from height h. After a time t, its speed is v = gt and it has fallen ½gt2. Its potential energy has dropped by mg × ½gt2 = ½mg2t2. Its kinetic energy has risen by ½m(gt)2 = ½mg2t2, exactly the same amount. So:

K + U = constant = mgh when only gravity does work (no friction, no air resistance)

A child at the top of a slide of height h has energy mgh. At the bottom (no friction) it has all become kinetic: ½mv2 = mgh, so v = √(2gh). The mass cancels, and the shape of the slide does not appear at all. Test that in the lab below.

Pendulum and real life

A pendulum bob swings back to almost the height it started from. At the ends it has only potential energy, and at the bottom only kinetic energy. In real life it slowly stops, because friction at the support and air resistance turn its energy into heat.

7.5 Power

Running up the stairs and walking up them slowly need the same work, because your weight is raised through the same height. But running feels very different. Power describes how fast the work is done:

P = W / t average power = work done ÷ time taken
  • SI unit: the watt (W). 1 W = 1 J s−1 (one joule of work every second).
  • More work in the same time, or the same work in less time, both need more power.
  • Car engines and pumps are often rated in horsepower: 1 hp = 746 W.
  • Careful: the symbol W means work, but the unit W means watt.

7.6 Simple machines

The total work needed for a job can't be reduced. But a simple machine can make the job easier by changing the size or the direction of the force you have to apply. The force you apply is the effort. The force to be overcome is the load.

mechanical advantage = load / effort MA greater than 1 means the machine lets a small effort move a big load

Pulley

A fixed pulley is a grooved wheel that guides a rope. It does not reduce the force (effort = load, so MA = 1). It just lets you pull down to lift something up, which is easier. Beyond the core: movable pulleys and systems of pulleys can have MA greater than 1, as in cranes and lifts.

Inclined plane

To get a load of weight mg up a height h, you can push it along a smooth ramp of length L instead of lifting it. If you push it at a steady speed with effort F′, then ignoring friction the work you do equals the potential energy it gains:

F′ × L = mgh  ⇒  MA = mg / F′ = L / h a longer, gentler ramp needs less effort, but over a longer distance

Lever

A lever is a rigid bar that turns about a fixed point, the fulcrum. The distance from the fulcrum to the load is the load arm. The distance from the fulcrum to the effort is the effort arm. The effort end moves a larger distance d1 with a small force F1. The load end moves a smaller distance d2 with a larger force F2. The work in equals the work out, F1 × d1 = F2 × d2, and the lever balances when:

effort × effort arm = load × load arm so MA = load / effort = effort arm / load arm

Beyond the core: classes of levers

Class I: fulcrum in the middle (seesaw, scissors, crowbar, pliers, beam balance). Class II: load in the middle (wheelbarrow, bottle opener, lemon squeezer). Class III: effort in the middle (tweezers, broom, hammer, oar).

Machines don't create energy. Ignoring friction, the work you put in equals the useful work done on the load. That is why no "perpetual motion machine" can ever work.

Worked examples, solved our way

These are the numerical examples in the chapter (Examples 7.1–7.13), solved step by step. Each one ends with a second way line: the same answer reached by a different route, so you can see it really is right.

Ex 7.1 Dumbbell lifted, then lowered slowly
  1. Going up: the girl's upward force and the displacement are both upwards, so she does positive work.
  2. Coming down slowly: she still pushes upwards, but the dumbbell moves down, opposite to her force, so she does negative work.
Ex 7.2 Goalkeeper's hands move back 15 cm with a 200 N force
  1. Convert: 15 cm = 0.15 m.
  2. The ball moves opposite to her force, so its displacement along the force is −0.15 m.
  3. W = F × s = 200 N × (−0.15 m).
W = −30 J (negative work on the ball)

the ball loses 30 J of kinetic energy while it is being stopped, which fits the work–energy theorem.

Ex 7.3 Carrom: striker → white coin → black coin

The striker pushes the white coin along the white coin's displacement, so it does positive work on it and the white coin gains energy. By Newton's third law, the white coin pushes back on the striker, against the striker's motion, so it does negative work on the striker and the striker loses energy. The same happens between the white coin (positive work on the black coin) and the black coin (negative work on the white coin).

Ex 7.4 Speed doubles: what happens to kinetic energy?
  1. Before: K = ½mv2.
  2. After: ½m(2v)2 = ½m × 4v2 = 4 × ½mv2.
4 times the original kinetic energy

try numbers: 1 kg at 2 m s−1 has 2 J, and at 4 m s−1 it has 8 J. 8 ÷ 2 = 4.

Ex 7.5 Cricket ball, 0.2 kg at 154.8 km h−1
  1. Convert to SI: 154.8 km h−1 = 154.8 × 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 43 m s−1.
  2. K = ½ × 0.2 kg × (43 m s−1)2 = 0.1 × 1849 J.
K = 184.9 J

dividing by 3.6 also converts km h−1 to m s−1: 154.8 ÷ 3.6 = 43 ✓.

Ex 7.6 Jet (15000 kg) stopped by a wire: 367500 N over 100 m
  1. The wire's force is backwards and the jet moves forwards, so the wire's work is negative: W = 367500 N × (−100 m) = −36750000 J.
  2. Change in kinetic energy = final − initial = 0 − ½ × 15000 kg × v2.
  3. Work–energy theorem: −7500 v2 = −36750000, so v2 = 4900 m2 s−2.
v = 70 m s⁻¹ (= 252 km h⁻¹)

with forces and motion: deceleration a = 367500 ÷ 15000 = 24.5 m s−2. Then v2 = 2as = 2 × 24.5 × 100 = 4900, so v = 70 m s−1 ✓.

Ex 7.7 200 g ball thrown 10 m up
  1. Convert: 200 g = 0.2 kg.
  2. U = mgh = 0.2 kg × 10 m s−2 × 10 m.
U = 20 J

to reach 10 m it must leave the hand at √(2gh) = √200 m s−1. That is a kinetic energy of ½ × 0.2 × 200 = 20 J ✓.

Ex 7.8 Speed at the bottom of a slide of height h
  1. Top: potential energy mgh, kinetic energy 0 (starts from rest).
  2. Bottom: potential energy 0, kinetic energy ½mv2. With no friction, nothing is lost.
  3. ½mv2 = mgh ⇒ v = √(2gh).
v = √(2gh): it doesn't depend on the child's mass or the slide's shape

a free fall from the same height gives v2 = 2gh from the equations of motion, the same answer. Lab 2 shows it for four different shapes.

Ex 7.9 Truck (10000 kg, 72 km h−1) on a 30° sand ramp, 50000 N resistance
  1. Speed: 72 km h−1 = 20 m s−1. Kinetic energy = ½ × 10000 × 202 = 2000000 J.
  2. Let the truck go a distance d up the ramp. On a 30° ramp it rises d/2, so it gains potential energy mg × d/2 = 10000 × 10 × d/2 = 50000d J.
  3. The sand does work −50000d J (force opposite to the motion).
  4. Work–energy theorem: −50000d = (0 + 50000d) − (2000000 + 0), so 100000d = 2000000.
d = 20 m (the minimum ramp length)

along the ramp, two things slow the truck: the sand (50000 N) and the part of gravity along the slope (mg/2 = 50000 N). The total is 100000 N, so 2000000 J ÷ 100000 N = 20 m ✓.

Ex 7.10 75 kg lifted 2 m in 5 s
  1. Work = mgh = 75 × 10 × 2 = 1500 J.
  2. P = W / t = 1500 J ÷ 5 s.
P = 300 W

force 750 N × average speed (2 m ÷ 5 s = 0.4 m s−1) = 300 W ✓.

Ex 7.11 Car (1000 kg) from rest to 72 km h−1 in 10 s
  1. v = 72 km h−1 = 20 m s−1, u = 0.
  2. Work by the engine = gain in kinetic energy = ½ × 1000 × 202 − 0 = 200000 J.
  3. P = 200000 J ÷ 10 s.
P = 20000 W (20 kW)

with steady acceleration: a = 20 ÷ 10 = 2 m s−2, F = ma = 2000 N, s = ½ × 2 × 102 = 100 m. Then W = 2000 × 100 = 200000 J ✓.

Ex 7.12 Ramp over a 30 cm step, 40 cm wide
  1. The ramp is the long side of a right-angled triangle: L = √(302 + 402) = √2500 = 50 cm.
  2. MA = L / h = 50 cm ÷ 30 cm = 5/3.
MA = 5/3 ≈ 1.67

effort F′ = load × h/L = 0.6 × load, and load ÷ (0.6 × load) = 1.67 ✓.

Ex 7.13 Seesaw seats at 2 m and 1 m, children of 15 kg and 30 kg
  1. Balance: (weight × distance) on one side = (weight × distance) on the other. Weight = mg and g cancels, so we can use the masses.
  2. Lighter child on the 2 m seat: 15 × 2 = 30 × L ⇒ L = 1 m.
15 kg child on an outer seat (2 m), 30 kg child on the inner seat on the other side (1 m)

15 kg × 2 m = 30 kg·m = 30 kg × 1 m ✓. The lighter child must sit farther out. Putting them the other way round (15 kg at 1 m) would need the 30 kg child at 0.5 m, and there is no seat there.

Pause and Ponder: short answers

Try each question in your book first, then check here (numbers 1–13 match the book).

1–3 Holding a barbell · friction on sliding coins · pedalling

1. No. Holding it steady means zero displacement, so no work is done on the barbell, even though she gets tired.

2. Negative. Friction acts opposite to the coins' displacement.

3. Chemical energy from food, through your muscles, becomes the kinetic energy of you and the bicycle. Some becomes heat (friction, air resistance) and a little becomes sound.

4–6 Same KE, different masses · constant velocity · moving sideways

4. ½mvA2 = ½(4m)vB2 ⇒ vA2 = 4vB2 ⇒ vA : vB = 2 : 1.

5. No. K = ½mv2 depends only on mass and speed, not on where the object is.

6. Moving horizontally, the height doesn't change, so the potential energy stays the same. Raised vertically, h increases, so U = mgh increases.

7–8 Just before hitting the ground · ball roller coaster

7. Just before landing, h′ = 0, so U = 0. Its speed satisfies v2 = 2gh, so K = ½m(2gh) = mgh. Mechanical energy = 0 + mgh = mgh.

8. High points have more potential energy and less kinetic energy. Low points have more kinetic energy and less potential energy. Each later hump is lower because friction (and air resistance) turns some mechanical energy into heat, so the ball can't climb back as high. Yes, it's friction.

9–10 Winding hill roads · inclined ladder

9. A road that winds up gently is a long inclined plane. A longer path L for the same rise h means a bigger MA = L/h, so vehicles need a smaller force to climb, although they travel farther.

10. An inclined ladder works like an inclined plane: you rise the same height over a longer path, so each step needs less effort than climbing straight up.

11–13 Spoon as a lid-opener · scissors · perpetual motion

11. The spoon is a lever. The rim of the can is the fulcrum, the short tip under the lid is the load arm, and the long handle is the effort arm. MA = effort arm ÷ load arm is large, so a small push gives a big force on the lid.

12. Near the fulcrum the load arm is short, so for the same effort the blades press much harder on the object (MA = effort arm ÷ load arm grows).

13. Friction and air resistance always do negative work on a moving machine and turn its mechanical energy into heat. A machine can't create energy, so with no fuel or electricity coming in, its energy runs out and it stops.

Q5 Student (50 kg) to the top of a 72.5 m building: lift vs stairs
  1. (i) Gain in potential energy = mgh = 50 × 10 × 72.5 J.
  2. (ii) By the stairs, the start and end heights are the same, so the gain is the same.
  3. (iii) Gravitational potential energy depends only on the height reached, not on the path taken.
36250 J in both cases

50 × 72.5 = 3625, and × 10 = 36250 ✓.

Q6 Crane: to the 10th floor in time t, then to the 20th floor in time 2t
  1. Let each floor be h0 high. Lifting to the 10th floor: W1 = mg × 10h0. To the 20th floor: W2 = mg × 20h0 = 2W1.
  2. Power: P1 = W1/t and P2 = 2W1/(2t) = P1.
Twice the energy (W more), but the same power

If you read the second trip as starting from the 10th floor instead, it lifts another 10 floors (energy W1, the same as the first trip) in time 2t, so it needs half the power. Either way, the energy depends only on the height lifted.

Q7 Raising a flag with a pulley: what decides the energy, and does speed matter?
  1. The energy needed is the work against gravity, mgh. It depends on the flag's mass (its weight) and the height of the pole (ignoring friction in the pulley).
  2. Slow or quick, the flag ends at the same height and at rest, so the work done is the same.
  3. Doubling the speed halves the time, so P = W/t doubles.
Work: the same · Power: doubles
Q8 Scooter: 160 kg on day 1, 200 kg on day 2, same speed
  1. Day 1 mass: 60 + 100 = 160 kg. Day 2: 160 + 40 = 200 kg.
  2. All the fuel's energy becomes kinetic energy ½Mv2. With the same v, the fuel used is proportional to M.
  3. Ratio = 160 : 200.
4 : 5

½ × 160 × v2 = 80v2 and ½ × 200 × v2 = 100v2. 80 : 100 = 4 : 5 ✓. The "same time" only matters for power, not for energy.

Q9 Seesaw: the adult weighs twice the child

Balance needs child's weight × child's distance = adult's weight × adult's distance. If the child weighs W and the adult 2W: W × dchild = 2W × dadult, so the child sits twice as far from the fulcrum as the adult.

child's distance = 2 × adult's distance (e.g. 2 m and 1 m)
Q10 2 kg ball thrown up at 20 m s−1, reaches 19.4 m
  1. (i) Going up, gravity (down) is opposite to the displacement (up), so its work is negative. Coming down, both point down, so its work is positive.
  2. (ii) Start: K = ½ × 2 × 202 = 400 J, U = 0. At the top: K = 0, U = 2 × 10 × 19.4 = 388 J.
  3. Mechanical energy fell from 400 J to 388 J. The only other force is air resistance, so its work = 388 − 400.
Work done by air resistance = −12 J

without air, the ball would rise to u2/2g = 400/20 = 20 m. It only reached 19.4 m, and the 0.6 m it "lost" × mg (20 N) = 12 J ✓.

Q11 10 kg block, variable force (graph), KE 180 J at 0 m

Reading the graph in the book: the force rises steadily from 0 to 50 N between 0 and 1 m, stays at 50 N from 1 m to 3 m, then falls steadily back to 0 at 4 m.

  1. (i) At 0 m: ½ × 10 × v2 = 180 ⇒ v2 = 36.
  2. Work from 0 to 4 m = area under the graph = triangle + rectangle + triangle = ½ × 1 × 50 + 2 × 50 + ½ × 1 × 50 = 25 + 100 + 25 = 150 J.
  3. (ii) K at 4 m = 180 + 150 = 330 J ⇒ 5v2 = 330 ⇒ v2 = 66.
speed at 0 m = 6 m s⁻¹ speed at 4 m = √66 ≈ 8.12 m s⁻¹

Negative acceleration? No. The force always points along the motion, or is zero at the very ends, and friction is negligible. So the block never slows down. It speeds up all the way, fastest-changing between 1 m and 3 m, where a = 50/10 = 5 m s−2.

trapezium area = ½ × (4 + 2) × 50 = 150 J ✓.

Q12 Ball thrown to 8 m on Earth: how high on the Moon?
  1. Same throwing speed u, so the same starting kinetic energy ½mu2.
  2. At the top it is all potential energy: mgh = ½mu2 ⇒ h = u2/2g. So h is proportional to 1/g.
  3. On the Moon g is ⅙ as big, so h is 6 times as big: 6 × 8 m.
48 m

on Earth, u2 = 2 × 10 × 8 = 160. On the Moon (g = 10/6), h = 160 ÷ (2 × 10/6) = 160 × 6/20 = 48 m ✓.

Q13 1000 kg car braking (speed–time graph A → B → C)

From the graph: A to B, the speed stays at 35 m s−1 for 1 s. B to C, it falls steadily to 0 by 3 s.

  1. (i) A to B: uniform motion at 35 m s−1, covering 35 m. This is the driver's reaction time, before the brakes act.
  2. (ii) K at A = ½ × 1000 × 352 = 500 × 1225 J.
  3. (iii) The car ends at rest, so the brakes' work = change in K = 0 − 612500 J (negative, because the braking force is opposite to the motion).
  4. (iv) Mostly into heat (thermal energy) in the brakes, tyres and road, plus a little sound.
K at A = 612500 J work by brakes = −612500 J

braking force = ma = 1000 × (35 ÷ 2) = 17500 N. Distance B to C = area of the triangle = ½ × 2 × 35 = 35 m. 17500 × 35 = 612500 J ✓.

Q14 0.5 kg ball on a frictionless track: speed at P, Q and R

From the graph: at O, U = 30 J and v = 0. At P, U = 20 J. At Q, U = 30 J. At R, U = 40 J.

  1. Total mechanical energy = K + U at O = 0 + 30 = 30 J. With no friction, it stays 30 J everywhere.
  2. P: K = 30 − 20 = 10 J ⇒ ½ × 0.5 × v2 = 10 ⇒ v2 = 40.
  3. Q: K = 30 − 30 = 0 ⇒ v = 0. The ball stops for an instant and rolls back.
  4. R: it would need U = 40 J, more than the 30 J it has. So the ball never reaches R. It turns back at Q.
P: √40 ≈ 6.32 m s⁻¹ Q: 0 m s⁻¹ R: not reached

at P, ½ × 0.5 × 6.322 ≈ 0.25 × 40 = 10 J, and 10 + 20 = 30 J ✓. Try the "Valley" track in Lab 2 with no friction: the ball never climbs above its start height.

Q15 1.5 kg coconut falls 10 m into sand (3000 N resistance)
  1. (i) ½mv2 = mgh ⇒ v = √(2 × 10 × 10) = √200 m s−1.
  2. (ii) Energy just before impact: ½ × 1.5 × 200 = 150 J (= mgh = 1.5 × 10 × 10 ✓).
  3. The sand stops it, doing work −3000 N × d. All 150 J is used up: 3000 × d = 150 ⇒ d = 0.05 m.
v = √200 ≈ 14.14 m s⁻¹ depth d = 0.05 m = 5 cm
Teacher's note

As the coconut sinks, gravity does a little extra work, mg × d = 15 N × d. Including it gives (3000 − 15)d = 150, so d ≈ 0.0503 m. That is still 5 cm to the accuracy of the data. The question tells us to use only the coconut's energy at impact, so 5 cm is the expected answer.

  • Square the speed in ½mv2, but not the mass.
  • Machines reduce the force, not the work.
